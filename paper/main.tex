\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Public disclosure overwhelmingly takes the form of PDFs, and a large
fraction of them resist reliable conversion into machine-searchable
text. The causes span a spectrum: incidental damage from scan-to-PDF
pipelines and technical debt at one end, deliberate obfuscation that
defeats automated readers while remaining visually intact at the other.
Whatever the cause, a document that cannot be reduced to faithful
plaintext --- a document you cannot \emph{grep} --- is, for practical
purposes, undisclosed. We present a reproducible synthetic benchmark
that quantifies this problem. One hundred public-domain source texts
are rendered to PDFs with byte-exact ground truth and then independently
poisoned with six obfuscation techniques, yielding a 700-PDF corpus.
We evaluate seven extractors --- five widely used open-source tools, an
open vision language model, and \mbox{\textsc{pat-cascade}}, a tiered
text-then-OCR cascade --- on both token-level F1 accuracy and wall-clock
runtime. \textsc{pat-cascade} is the only extractor that scores at or
above $0.986$ on every variant (mean $0.995$), while each single-pass
tool has at least one technique that defeats it outright. Runtime spans
more than four orders of magnitude; the cascade occupies the pragmatic
middle, paying optical-character-recognition (OCR) cost only when its
garble detector escalates. All code and data are public.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{The problem}

The Portable Document Format is a \emph{layout} format. A PDF page is a
content stream of drawing operators that place glyphs, images, and
vector graphics at coordinates; the format makes no promise that the
sequence of glyphs a reader sees corresponds in any recoverable way to a
sequence of Unicode characters~\cite{pdfspec}. The mapping from a drawn
glyph back to a character is carried by an \emph{optional}
\texttt{ToUnicode} CMap. Text can be drawn in an invisible rendering
mode, drawn off the page, or replaced wholesale by a rasterized image.
``What the page shows'' and ``what the text layer says'' are therefore
free to diverge arbitrarily.

In practice they diverge constantly. A government office scans a paper
filing and posts the image-only result. A converter embeds a subsetted
font with no \texttt{ToUnicode} table. A template stamps a
\texttt{CONFIDENTIAL} watermark across every page. Some of this is
incidental --- technical debt, a misconfigured pipeline, an accident of
tooling. Some of it is deliberate: patterns that pass a human reader's
glance but defeat OCR, text-layer parsers, and language-model-based
extractors. From an extraction pipeline's point of view the
\emph{intent} does not matter. What matters is that the document
resists being turned into faithful, searchable plaintext.

That capability is valuable. Auditing a corpus of disclosures, building
a full-text index, running a regular expression across a regulatory
filing set --- all of it presupposes that each PDF can be reduced to the
text it represents, with high fidelity. When extraction silently returns
empty strings, scrambled codepoints, or attacker-controlled payloads,
every downstream analysis inherits the corruption. This paper measures
how far common tools fall short, and what it costs to do better.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item A \textbf{taxonomy} of twelve structural PDF obfuscation
    techniques, each paired with a cheap static-analysis detection
    signal (Section~\ref{sec:threat}).
  \item A \textbf{reproducible synthetic benchmark}: 100 public-domain
    source texts $\times$ 7 variants (one clean baseline plus six
    independent obfuscation techniques) $=$ 700 PDFs, every variant
    anchored to byte-exact ground truth (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
  \item A head-to-head \textbf{evaluation of seven extractors} ---
    including the proposed \textsc{pat-cascade} --- on both accuracy and
    runtime, spanning more than four orders of magnitude of compute cost
    (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
  \item A \textbf{visual demonstration} of each technique, contrasting
    the page a human sees with the text a machine recovers
    (Figures~\ref{fig:watermark}--\ref{fig:invisible_text}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Research questions}

The benchmark is organized around two questions. \textbf{RQ1:} what does
PDF obfuscation look like, structurally, and can each technique be
detected cheaply? \textbf{RQ2:} how badly does each technique break each
common extractor, and at what compute cost is the damage recovered?
Measuring how often these techniques occur in real-world corpora is left
to future work (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}); this paper establishes
the controlled, ground-truth-anchored baseline that such a study would
build on.

This is a pre-print. The results for the six classic extractors are at
the full $n=100$ scale and we consider them final; the vision-language
model column is reported on an $n=10$ subset, with the full-scale run
forthcoming (Section~\ref{sec:olmocr}).

\section{Threat model: a taxonomy of PDF obfuscation}
\label{sec:threat}

\subsection{Why PDFs resist text extraction}

Because the glyph-to-character mapping is optional and the content
stream is unconstrained, a PDF can withhold its text from automated
extraction in structurally distinct ways. An extractor that only walks
the text layer and one that only reads rendered pixels have disjoint
blind spots: the first is defeated by image-only pages, the second by
text whose visual form has been decoupled from its codepoints. A
realistic threat model therefore has to enumerate techniques
\emph{by mechanism}, not by severity alone.

\subsection{The taxonomy}

Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy} lists twelve techniques. For each we give the
effect on extraction and a detection signal that runs cheaply as a
static-analysis pass over the PDF structure --- no rendering, no OCR.
The detection signals answer RQ1: every technique in the table leaves a
structural fingerprint. Six of the twelve are realized as synthetic
variants in this benchmark (marked~$\dagger$); the rest are documented
for completeness and are targets for future benchmark coverage.

\subsection{Six techniques in depth}
\label{sec:six}

The six benchmarked techniques are applied independently to each clean
source PDF. We describe each and, in
Figures~\ref{fig:watermark}--\ref{fig:invisible_text}, demonstrate it
on a representative document --- a U.S.\ Federal Register rule --- by
contrasting the rendered page with the output of a naive text-layer
extractor. (Token-F1, the accuracy metric quoted in the figures, is
defined in Section~\ref{sec:scoring}.) The crucial observation is that
\emph{four of the six techniques leave the rendered page pixel-identical
to the clean document}: the human-facing artifact is unchanged while the
machine-facing text is corrupted or destroyed.

\paragraph{Watermark.} A diagonal, semi-transparent
\texttt{CONFIDENTIAL\,--\,DRAFT} overlay is stamped on every page. The
body text still extracts, but the overlay's glyphs are merged into the
content stream and leak into the text layer once per page
(Figure~\ref{fig:watermark}). The damage is pollution, not destruction:
downstream search and tokenization see spurious tokens on every page.

\obfusfig{watermark}{watermark_page.png}{watermark_extracted.txt}{%
  \variant{watermark}. A diagonal overlay is stamped on every page. The
  body text is recovered, but the overlay string is merged into the
  content stream and leaks into the text layer once per page.}

\paragraph{Metadata swap.} The document's \texttt{Producer},
\texttt{Creator}, and \texttt{Title} are rewritten to a known-bad
scan-pipeline signature. No extractor's output changes --- metadata is
not part of any page's text --- so this technique is invisible to
extraction by construction (Figure~\ref{fig:metadata_swap}). It is
included precisely to exercise the gap between \emph{extraction} and
\emph{provenance}: the static fingerprinter catches it at 100\%
precision (Section~\ref{sec:detect}) while every extractor is blind.

\obfusfig{metadata_swap}{clean_page.png}{metadata_swap_extracted.txt}{%
  \variant{metadata\_swap}. The rendered page is identical to the clean
  document and the extracted text is byte-identical: no extractor sees
  this attack. The poisoning lives entirely in the document metadata,
  where the static fingerprinter catches it.}

\paragraph{Rasterize.} Each page is re-rendered to a 150-DPI image and
the text layer is discarded, leaving an image-only PDF. This is the most
adversarial technique for text-layer tools: the content stream contains
no text-showing operators at all, so \texttt{pypdf}, \texttt{pdfplumber}
and \texttt{pdftotext} return the empty string and score exactly $0.000$
(Figure~\ref{fig:rasterize}). Only OCR or a vision model can recover the
content. The rendered page is, by construction, visually identical to
the original.

\obfusfig{rasterize}{clean_page.png}{rasterize_extracted.txt}{%
  \variant{rasterize}. The page is re-rendered as an image and the text
  layer discarded. It looks identical to the clean document, but every
  text-layer extractor returns the empty string. Only OCR or a vision
  model can recover the content.}

\paragraph{Homoglyph substitution.} Roughly one in three eligible Latin
letters is replaced with a visually identical Cyrillic codepoint --- for
example the Latin small letter \texttt{a} (U+0061) becomes Cyrillic
\texttt{a} (U+0430) --- and the text is re-rendered with a Unicode-capable
font. The page is pixel-identical to the clean version; the extracted
text layer, however, is a mixed Latin/Cyrillic codepoint stream that
defeats exact string search and Unicode-naive tokenization
(Figure~\ref{fig:homoglyph}). OCR, which reads pixels, recovers the
Latin form.

\obfusfig{homoglyph}{clean_page.png}{homoglyph_extracted.txt}{%
  \variant{homoglyph}. About one Latin letter in three is replaced by a
  visually identical Cyrillic codepoint. The page is pixel-identical to
  the clean document; the extracted text layer is a poisoned codepoint
  stream that defeats exact string search.}

\paragraph{Character spacing.} An abnormal \texttt{Tc}
(character-spacing) operator is injected into each page's content
stream, widening every inter-glyph gap past the width of the glyph
itself. Extractors that infer word boundaries from glyph spacing read
the result as ``\texttt{h e l l o}'' --- one token per character
(Figure~\ref{fig:char_spacing}). Notably, the rendered page \emph{is}
visibly altered here, and extractors that reconstruct words from glyph
\emph{positions} rather than gaps are unaffected.

\obfusfig{char_spacing}{char_spacing_page.png}{char_spacing_extracted.txt}{%
  \variant{char\_spacing}. An abnormal \texttt{Tc} operator widens every
  inter-glyph gap. Extractors that infer word boundaries from gaps read
  one token per glyph; \texttt{pypdf}, which reconstructs words from
  glyph positions, is unaffected.}

\paragraph{Invisible text.} A short payload --- here a
prompt-injection string --- is appended to the last page in PDF
rendering mode~3, which draws no pixels. A human reader sees nothing;
a text-layer extractor reads the payload back verbatim
(Figure~\ref{fig:invisible_text}). This is the indirect
prompt-injection vector~\cite{greshake2023} in PDF form: an attacker
controls text that never appears on screen but lands in every
downstream pipeline.

\obfusfig{invisible_text}{clean_page.png}{invisible_text_extracted.txt}{%
  \variant{invisible\_text}. A payload is drawn in invisible rendering
  mode on the last page. The page looks identical to the clean
  document; a text-layer extractor reads the payload back verbatim.}

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Synthetic gold set}

The benchmark uses 100 source texts, each an excerpt targeting roughly
1\,500 words and rendering to 3--5 pages (mean 4.8 pages per clean PDF).
Sources are balanced across five public-domain or CC-BY-SA-compatible
pools, about twenty documents each:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{gutenberg} --- pre-1930 literary excerpts from Project
    Gutenberg;
  \item \textbf{federal-register} --- U.S.\ federal rules and notices
    via the Federal Register API, spanning 17 agencies;
  \item \textbf{usc} --- United States Code sections, 16 distinct
    titles;
  \item \textbf{wikipedia} --- English Wikipedia article extracts in
    science, history, geography, and engineering;
  \item \textbf{agency-pubs} --- U.S.\ federal-agency factsheets (CDC,
    NIH, NIST, NOAA, USGS, EPA, FDA, and others).
\end{itemize}

Curation, deduplication, and SHA-256 provenance manifests are tracked
with the corpus. To keep the v1 dataset focused, the renderer restricts
input to Latin script plus standard punctuation: non-Latin codepoints
are substituted out at source-write time. This is a deliberate scoping
decision --- it lets the benchmark measure extraction quality without
confounding it with non-Latin script and bidirectional-layout handling
--- and is revisited in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Poisoning pipeline}

Clean PDFs are rendered deterministically (reportlab~\cite{reportlab}
with an invariant canvas, so re-renders are byte-identical) from the
source text. Each clean PDF is then poisoned, independently, by each of
the six techniques of Section~\ref{sec:six}, producing six variants per
source. Post-render techniques (\variant{watermark},
\variant{metadata\_swap}, \variant{rasterize}, \variant{char\_spacing})
operate on the clean PDF structurally, using pikepdf~\cite{pikepdf} for
content-stream and metadata edits and pypdfium2~\cite{pypdfium2} for
page rasterization; pre-render techniques (\variant{homoglyph},
\variant{invisible\_text}) re-render from adjusted source text or render
options. Because the source text is unchanged, every poisoned variant
retains a byte-exact ground-truth reference. The technique catalogue
draws on the open-source PDF-Poisoning project~\cite{pdfpoisoning};
each technique is reimplemented natively against the deterministic
clean PDFs so that the synthetic pipeline stays reproducible and
dependency-light.

\subsection{Ground truth and round-trip exactness}
\label{sec:groundtruth}

Ground truth for every variant is the normalized source text. We
validate the harness with a round-trip control on the clean PDFs:
source text $\rightarrow$ clean PDF $\rightarrow$ \texttt{pypdf}
$\rightarrow$ normalize, compared against the normalized source. The
result is \textbf{95 of 100} byte-exact and \textbf{100 of 100} at
token-F1 $\geq 0.99$. The five imperfect cases share one cause: Federal
Register documents containing very long URLs that the renderer's
paragraph engine breaks mid-token, inserting a single space (an F1 loss
of $\approx 0.001$). The harness control is therefore tight enough that
any larger accuracy gap in the results is a genuine extraction effect,
not a measurement artifact. Figure~\ref{fig:clean} shows the control on
the representative document.

\subsection{Extractors evaluated}
\label{sec:extractors}

Seven extractors are evaluated through a uniform interface. Six are
classic text-layer or OCR tools:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{pypdf}~\cite{pypdf} --- pure-Python text-layer
    extraction; no OCR.
  \item \textbf{pdfplumber}~\cite{pdfplumber} --- text-layer extraction
    with a layout-aware positioning model.
  \item \textbf{pdftotext}~\cite{poppler} --- the poppler command-line
    text-layer extractor.
  \item \textbf{Tesseract}~\cite{tesseract} --- pixel-based OCR; pages
    are rasterized via pypdfium2 and recognized with the Tesseract
    engine.
  \item \textbf{Docling}~\cite{docling} --- an integrated converter
    combining text-layer parsing with a built-in
    RapidOCR~\cite{rapidocr} fallback; an ``all-in-one'' choice.
  \item \textbf{OlmOCR-2}~\cite{olmocr} --- an open vision language
    model (a Qwen2.5-VL-7B fine-tune~\cite{qwen25vl}); see
    Section~\ref{sec:olmocr}.
\end{itemize}

The seventh, \textsc{pat-cascade}, is the proposed method: a
tiered cascade from the Personal Advocacy Toolkit~\cite{pat}. It begins
with fast text-layer extraction and escalates to OCR only when a
garble detector flags the text-layer output as unusable --- the design
intent being to pay OCR cost on the documents that need it and
text-layer speed on the documents that do not. It is invoked as a
subprocess so that the same binary a downstream consumer would deploy is
the one measured. Cloud vision-LLM wrappers (Claude, Gemini) are wired
into the harness but were skipped this run pending an API budget; see
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Scoring}
\label{sec:scoring}

The primary metric is \textbf{token-level F1}: extracted text and ground
truth are NFC-normalized, whitespace-collapsed, and compared as bags of
tokens, with F1 the harmonic mean of token precision and recall. We also
report \textbf{normalized edit distance} (NED) for the vision-LLM
column. Token-F1 is robust to benign reordering and rewards partial
recovery, which makes it a fair common currency across tools as
different as a text-layer parser and a vision model.

\subsection{Runtime measurement}
\label{sec:runtimemethod}

Accuracy is measured at the full $n=100$ scale. Runtime is measured
separately, on a fixed 10-source subset (10 sources $\times$ 7 variants
$=$ 70 PDFs, 399 page renders), with each extractor timed in its own
pass, one PDF at a time, on a single Apple M2 (16\,GB). Isolating the
timing run this way --- rather than reading the per-PDF times recorded
incidentally during the accuracy run --- means a claim such as
``extractor $X$ is $N\times$ slower than \texttt{pypdf}'' is a
defensible number rather than an artifact of scheduling. The same
10-source subset is used for the vision-LLM column so that its accuracy
and runtime are mutually consistent.

\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Accuracy}
\label{sec:accuracy}

Seven observations follow from the table.

\textbf{(1) The harness control passes.} On the \variant{clean} column
\texttt{pypdf}, \texttt{pdfplumber}, and \textsc{pat-cascade} hit
$1.000$ and \texttt{pdftotext} reaches $0.998$. The $0.986$ for
Tesseract and Docling reflects systematic OCR and markdown-serialization
noise, not extraction failure. Per-pool variation is small ($1.000 \pm
0.006$ for text-layer tools across all five pools), so any larger gap
elsewhere in the table is a real extraction effect.

\textbf{(2) The cascade wins or ties every column.}
\textsc{pat-cascade} scores $\geq 0.986$ on every variant, including the
two --- \variant{rasterize} and \variant{homoglyph} --- that defeat
every other text-layer tool, for a mean of $0.995$. No other extractor
exceeds $0.842$.

\textbf{(3) Tesseract is the only \emph{single-pass} tool that beats
both \variant{rasterize} and \variant{homoglyph}}, at $0.986$ on each,
because OCR reads rendered pixels rather than codepoints: a rasterized
page is its native input, and Cyrillic homoglyphs render as Latin-looking
glyphs and are recognized as Latin. The trade-off appears on
\variant{watermark} ($0.849$) and \variant{char\_spacing} ($0.085$),
where overlay noise and wide inter-character gaps confuse the
line/word segmenter.

\textbf{(4) Docling sits between Tesseract and the text-layer tools.}
Its RapidOCR fallback yields $0.840$ on \variant{rasterize} --- better
than the $0.000$ of pure text-layer tools, worse than Tesseract's
$0.986$ because Docling's markdown post-processing adds serialization
noise. It is the easiest single-tool choice, but a cascade that
explicitly escalates to OCR beats it on every column.

\textbf{(5) \variant{char\_spacing} splits the text-layer tools in
two.} \texttt{pypdf} and \textsc{pat-cascade} score $1.000$ because they
reconstruct words from glyph \emph{positions} and ignore the \texttt{Tc}
operator. \texttt{pdftotext} ($0.017$), \texttt{pdfplumber} ($0.010$),
Docling ($0.016$), and Tesseract ($0.085$) read the wide gaps as word
boundaries and emit one token per glyph.

\textbf{(6) \variant{metadata\_swap} is invisible to every extractor}
(all $\geq 0.986$, matching each tool's \variant{clean} score). Pure
text extraction ignores document metadata. The technique is included to
mark the boundary of what extraction can see --- and what a structural
fingerprinter must cover instead (Section~\ref{sec:detect}).

\textbf{(7) \variant{invisible\_text} barely moves token-F1}
($0.985$--$0.999$). The synthetic payload is a single
$\approx$70-character overlay --- under 2\% of a 1\,500-word excerpt ---
so even when an extractor ingests it the F1 drops only $0.001$--$0.014$.
The technique's danger is qualitative (attacker-controlled content in
the pipeline), not a large F1 deflection; a real-world payload would be
larger.

\subsection{OlmOCR-2: an open vision language model}

\subsection{The accuracy/runtime trade-off}
\label{sec:tradeoff}

Pairing accuracy with runtime makes the design space concrete.
\texttt{pypdf} extracts a page in 3\,ms but scores $0.000$ on
\variant{rasterize}; OlmOCR-2 scores $0.989$ on \variant{rasterize} but
costs 74\,s/page --- roughly $24{,}000\times$ more. \textsc{pat-cascade}
is the pragmatic middle: text-layer speed ($\approx 0.2$\,s/PDF) on the
documents whose text layer is usable, escalating to OCR cost only on the
poisoned variants that need it, for $\geq 0.986$ accuracy across the
board at a 3.74\,s/PDF blended mean. Paying for OCR unconditionally
(Tesseract, Docling) or for a vision model unconditionally (OlmOCR) buys
robustness on the hard variants at a flat cost on the easy ones; the
cascade buys the same robustness and spends the cost only where the
document demands it.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{A tiered cascade dominates the accuracy frontier.}
\textsc{pat-cascade} is the only extractor that never falls below
$0.986$. Every single-pass tool has at least one technique that defeats
it: text-layer tools collapse to $0.000$ on \variant{rasterize};
Tesseract collapses to $0.085$ on \variant{char\_spacing}; Docling drops
to $0.489$ on \variant{homoglyph}. The cascade avoids each cliff by not
committing to a single mechanism --- it reads the text layer first, and
escalates to OCR exactly when a garble detector reports the text-layer
result is unusable.

\textbf{The escalation is what makes the cost acceptable.} A naive
``always OCR'' policy reaches comparable robustness, but
Table~\ref{tab:runtimevariant} shows it pays the OCR bill on every
document. The cascade pays it twice in seven variants and runs at
text-layer speed on the other five. The cost of robustness is thus
proportional to how poisoned the corpus actually is, rather than flat.

\textbf{The cascade and the vision model occupy different niches.}
OlmOCR-2 matches the cascade on the pixel-invariant attacks and, like
Tesseract, is immune to codepoint tricks --- but it collapses on
\variant{char\_spacing} ($0.618$ vs.\ the cascade's $1.000$), because a
vision model has no glyph-position channel to fall back on, and it costs
four orders of magnitude more per page. A vision LLM is the right tool
for documents that are purely image-based or codepoint-poisoned and for
which no text layer exists to reconstruct from; it is the wrong tool for
a corpus that is mostly clean.

\textbf{Remaining headroom.} The cascade's two lowest scores are
\variant{homoglyph} and \variant{rasterize}, both $0.986$, where it
relies on its OCR tier. A cheaper path on \variant{homoglyph} ---
detecting non-Latin-in-Latin codepoints at the text-layer stage and
correcting them without a full OCR pass --- would lift accuracy and cut
the 20\,s/PDF escalation cost at the same time. The fingerprinter of
Section~\ref{sec:detect} already produces exactly that signal.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{The vision-LLM column is $n=10$.} As stated in
Section~\ref{sec:olmocr}, OlmOCR-2 is reported on a 10-source subset and
is not yet cell-for-cell comparable with the $n=100$ table. The
full-scale run is forthcoming.

\textbf{No frontier vision-LLM numbers.} Claude and Gemini vision
wrappers are wired into the harness but were not run; the gap is an API
budget, estimated at roughly \$20 for a full 700-PDF pass.

\textbf{Latin script only.} Non-Latin codepoints are substituted out of
the sources, so the benchmark measures Latin-script extraction. How
OCR-based and text-layer tools handle CJK, Arabic, Hebrew, and other
scripts is a separate axis that a Unicode-aware renderer and a
bidirectional-layout pool would isolate.

\textbf{Synthetic, not in-the-wild.} The corpus is synthetic by design
--- that is what buys byte-exact ground truth --- so it measures
\emph{susceptibility}, not \emph{prevalence}. How often each technique
occurs in real document corpora is unmeasured here.

\textbf{Modest payloads.} The \variant{invisible\_text} payload is a
single short string; a larger payload would deflect token-F1 more and
should be tracked when real-document data is available.

\section{Conclusion and future work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

PDFs that look ordinary on screen routinely resist automated parsing,
and a document that cannot be reduced to faithful plaintext is, for any
search or audit pipeline, effectively undisclosed. We built a
reproducible synthetic benchmark --- 100 sources, seven variants,
byte-exact ground truth --- that quantifies exactly how far common tools
fall short. Every single-pass extractor has a blind spot; a tiered
text-then-OCR cascade, \textsc{pat-cascade}, is the only one of seven
extractors that stays at or above $0.986$ token-F1 on every obfuscation
technique, and it does so at a blended 3.74\,s/PDF by escalating to OCR
only when needed. A static fingerprinter catches all six techniques
structurally, including the one invisible to every extractor.

Four directions follow. First, scale the OlmOCR-2 column from $n=10$ to
the full $n=100$ so it shares the main results table. Second, run the
frontier cloud vision LLMs on the same corpus. Third, add a non-Latin
script pool with a Unicode-aware renderer to separate extraction quality
from script handling. Fourth, measure technique prevalence on real-world
document corpora, using this synthetic benchmark as the
ground-truth-anchored baseline.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.} The benchmark harness, the synthesis and
poisoning pipeline, and the obfuscation fingerprinter are released as
open source (code under MIT, dataset and documentation under
CC-BY-4.0).\footnote{\url{https://github.com/EvanOchsner/pdf-plaintext-extraction}}
The full $n=100$ run is reproducible end-to-end with a single command,
\texttt{run\_synthetic}; the figures in this paper are regenerated by
the script under \texttt{paper/figures/}.
